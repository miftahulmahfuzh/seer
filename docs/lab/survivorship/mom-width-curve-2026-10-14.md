# The account's relative-strength book, at five widths, on both price histories

**2026-10-14.** Report only on the survivorship half: the re-reads on both histories recorded no
trial, took no test-window look, and left the lab's luck count where they found it. The five
widths themselves were run as ordinary dev trials and are recorded as such.

## 1. What this measures, and why it had to be measured

The account holds two books and the owner mirrors both with his own money. The first — picked by
residual momentum — was measured at seven widths on both price histories a day earlier, and the
answer was to widen it from twenty names to thirty-five.

This is the other one. It picks on relative strength and de-risks only when its own swings are
wider than its own normal. Until today it had never been read against the price history that keeps
the companies that went bankrupt, were bought or were renamed; it had no row in the committed
survivorship grid; and it had never once been run at the fees the owner actually pays, in the
fractional shares he actually buys, on the monthly top-up plan he actually follows. Its recorded
runs were whole shares, a flat fee, and no deposits at all. So nothing the lab held was a
measurement of what half his money was doing.

Both holes are now closed.

## 2. The headline

**Twenty names — the width he already holds — is the best of the five on the honest history.** It
falls 20.1% where the widest of the five falls 18.5%, and it earns 12.5% a year where the widest
earns 10.3%. Widening this book buys about a point and a half of safety across the entire sweep
and costs about two points a year to get it.

**That is the opposite of the other book's answer, and the opposite of what this method predicted
before it ran.** Both facts are stated plainly below rather than smoothed over.

The comparison that matters most: at the twenty names the owner already holds, this book is as
safe on the honest history as the other book becomes only after widening it to thirty-five
(20.1% against 20.1%). The two books do not carry the same single-name risk, and they do not want
the same width.

Nothing here says the picking rule is good. This line already failed out of sample and promotion
was refused on kin before the run started. The question asked and answered was narrower: what does
it cost to hold this book in twenty slots rather than more.

## 3. Why this file is separate from the committed survivorship grid

The committed grid is not appended to here. The hard gate's single-name-risk condition reads that
exact file, so adding rows to it would change which promotions the gate refuses as a side effect
of a research run. New measurements land as their own dated files. That is the same reason the
other book's curve has its own dated file, and this upholds a decision made on 2026-10-14 rather
than re-opening it.

## 4. The paired curve

Worst fall is the daily figure. Return is money-weighted on the owner's real plan — 10,000,000
rupiah to open and 5,000,000 on the 25th of every month, 237 deposits — measured against a market
fund fed the identical deposits on the identical days. Fractional shares, Gotrade's real fees.
Every arm's re-run on our usual history reproduced its recorded trial.

| names | fall, usual | fall, honest | gap | a year, usual | a year, honest | market | return ÷ fall, honest | trades |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **20** | 19.0% | **20.1%** | +1.1 | 11.2% | **12.5%** | 7.2% | **0.62** | 1,222 |
| 25 | 19.2% | 21.0% | +1.8 | 10.8% | 11.4% | 7.2% | 0.54 | 1,460 |
| 30 | 19.3% | 19.2% | −0.1 | 10.1% | 10.8% | 7.2% | 0.56 | 1,774 |
| 35 | 19.3% | 19.0% | −0.3 | 9.3% | 10.8% | 7.2% | 0.57 | 2,026 |
| 45 | 19.4% | 18.5% | −0.9 | 9.2% | 10.3% | 7.2% | 0.56 | 2,431 |

Three things to read off it.

**Our usual history is blind here, as it was for the other book.** All five falls sit between 19.0%
and 19.4% — a quarter of a point apart across a book that more than doubles in width. It cannot
see what width does, only what width costs.

**Widening does lower the fall on the honest history, but barely** — 20.1% to 18.5% across the
whole sweep, about a point and a half, and not in a straight line: twenty-five names is the worst
of the five.

**Widening costs real return.** 12.5% a year at twenty, 10.3% at forty-five. Two points a year,
paid to remove a point and a half of a fall that was never near the limit.

**Every arm earns more on the honest history than on our usual one**, by half a point to a point
and a half a year, and every arm wins a majority of the four walk-forward stretches there where
it wins only half on our usual history. The companies our usual history cannot price were, for
this book, more often bought or merged than bankrupt.

## 5. When the worst moment happened

Peak and trough months, and the worst fall inside each stretch, on the honest history.

| names | peak | trough | 1998 | 2000–02 | 2006 | 2008–09 | 2010–15 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 1998-06 | **1998-08** | 14.4% | 7.9% | 14.3% | 6.0% | 10.5% |
| 25 | 2006-03 | **2006-08** | 14.1% | 7.7% | 14.6% | 6.0% | 11.2% |
| 30 | 1998-06 | **1998-08** | 13.4% | 5.5% | 13.1% | 5.6% | 10.1% |
| 35 | 1998-06 | **1998-08** | 14.3% | 5.2% | 12.4% | 4.8% | 10.0% |
| 45 | 1998-06 | **1998-08** | 14.6% | 5.7% | 9.9% | 4.6% | 10.1% |

**This is the explanation of the whole result, and it was not the one predicted.** Four of the
five widths have their worst moment in the summer of 1998 — a market-wide fall that hit everything
at once. The other book's worst moment at twenty names was the spring of 2006, when the market
dipped two percent and recovered to a new high by September while a few of its holdings came apart
on their own. Those are different accidents, and only one of them is the kind that holding more
names can fix.

So the two books want different widths for a reason that can be dated. Spreading money over more
companies protects against one company failing by itself. It does nothing about a month when
everything falls together — and that, for this book, is the worst month there is.

The 2006 column is the one place width clearly works here: 14.3% at twenty names down to 9.9% at
forty-five. But for this book 2006 was never the worst episode, so fixing it does not lower the
worst fall. At twenty names 1998 and 2006 are within a tenth of a point of each other, which is
why twenty-five names reads worse than twenty — widening shaved 2006 just enough to leave 1998
standing alone, and 1998 is not shaved by width at all.

**How to read these numbers, and how not to.** Peak and trough months and the five stretch columns
are computed from month-end balances, so each is a floor on the true fall inside that stretch —
compare the 14.4% above with the 20.1% daily figure in the previous table. They are sound for
telling one stretch from another, which is all they are used for here.

How far they can be trusted was measured today rather than guessed, by re-running the other book's
seven widths under a single convention. **The 2006 column is sound**: re-measured and published
figures agree to within three ten-thousandths at every width, so the 2006 readings above — the one
place width visibly works for this book — can be compared across the two books directly. **The
whole-window 1998 figure is convention-sensitive**, by up to three and a half thousandths,
depending on whether balances are de-funded before or after month-ends are sampled. That is a
difference in bookkeeping convention, not in measurement, and it is small enough that it cannot
move which stretch holds a given book's worst moment — which is the only load this file puts on
it. The daily falls, the returns and the trade counts, which carry the conclusion, are unaffected
and reproduce exactly.

## 7. The recommendation

**Twenty names. Leave this book where it is.**

This follows the decision rule written down before the run, without departing from it. That rule
said: take the narrowest width whose fall on the honest history is at or under 23.0% — the limit
less a two-point cushion, because the honest history is a cross-check and not truth — provided its
return is within one point a year of the twenty-name arm's. It added that if the twenty-name arm
itself cleared 23.0%, the answer was twenty and the finding that the two books differ was to be
reported as the primary result rather than as an absence.

Twenty clears at 20.1%, with nearly five points of room under the limit and three under the
cushioned threshold. It is also the best of the five on return and the best on return-per-unit-of-
fall. No other rung is close on any measure the rule cares about, so the answer does not depend on
the cushion, on the tie-break, or on which of the two histories is believed.

**Two of this method's own predictions were wrong, and in the owner's favour.** It predicted the
twenty-name fall would land between 23% and 28% on the honest history; it is 20.1%. It predicted
the gap between the two histories would open between two and six points at twenty names and close
by forty-five; it opens one point and then goes *negative*. And it predicted widening from twenty
to thirty-five would cost under half a point a year; it costs one and three quarters. The method
expected to find a book in trouble that width would rescue. It found a book that is fine and that
width would make worse.

**What this does not say.** It is not a recommendation to keep holding this book. The line it comes
from already failed out of sample, promotion was refused before the run on those grounds, and
nothing measured here speaks to whether the picking rule works. It answers only the width question
it was asked.

**What to do about it.** Nothing, to this book. The account's other book is being widened to
thirty-five names on its own separate evidence; this one stays at twenty. That is a change to one
of the two books and not to both, which is the right outcome and not an oversight.

## 8. Limits

- **The honest history is a cross-check, not truth.** It adds most of the companies our usual
  history cannot price and not all of them, and genuine collapses were kept while data errors were
  cleaned, which is a judgement call. A conclusion that turns on a few tenths of a point on it
  turns on which errors somebody chose to clean. This one does not: twenty names wins by five
  points of fall and two points a year.
- **Month-end granularity.** The era figures are computed from month-end equity, so each is a
  floor on the true daily fall inside that stretch, not the fall itself.
- **This is a width finding, not a verdict on the picking rule.** The line runs through a book
  that already failed out of sample. Nothing here says the rule works; it says only what it costs
  to hold it in twenty slots rather than more.
- **Why this book's falls barely move with width is dated but not fully explained.** The dates
  above establish *that* its worst moment is a market-wide month rather than a single company
  coming apart, which is sufficient to settle the width question. A further possibility is that
  this book's own safety rule — it steps back whenever its swings widen beyond its own normal —
  is already absorbing the single-company damage that holding more names would otherwise absorb.
  That would explain why its falls are flat across width, and it remains the leading explanation,
  but it is **not** established here and should not be quoted as a result; measuring how much of
  the window each width spends stepped back would settle it, and that is a separate question. A
  parallel measurement the same day does remove one competing explanation: trimming individual
  holdings as they fall behind cannot be what closes the gap, because that test and a top-twenty
  momentum screen select almost entirely different companies — in the spring of 2006 it flagged
  thirty-six names and the book held none of them.
- **The live roster entry trades a different rule set.** The account's relative-strength entry
  runs on a tape whose top-up band is read against the account's own size and, measured
  2026-10-14, never fires in a many-name book — so the owner's monthly deposits reach it only
  through turnover. The tape measured here is the one that actually invests them, and is the tape
  the other book's whole curve was measured on. The size of that difference for this book is not
  measured here.
