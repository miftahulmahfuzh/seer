Two books were drawn this week that match the book they control on both of the things the
lab checks: how often it trades, and how much money it moves. One of them, against the top
fifty by company size, matches to within one per cent on both axes. It then earned four
points a year MORE than the book it was drawn to test.

That is not evidence that picking names blindly beats ranking them by size, and nobody
should read it that way.

The reason is in how the two books hold their money, which is the one thing nobody matched.
A blind control hands each of its fifty names a slot decided once, by a fixed rule, and it
re-imposes those slots every month. So every month it sells a little of whatever went up
and buys a little of whatever went down. The book it controls does the opposite by
construction: a book weighted by company size lets its biggest companies grow into bigger
slots and never trims them. Re-balancing like that is a source of return in its own right,
quite separate from which names are held.

This lab has priced that effect independently, with no control in it at all. An equal-weight
version of the same top fifty holds the IDENTICAL fifty names and records the IDENTICAL 360
trades as the size-weighted one, and earns 6.9% a year against 5.6% - one and a quarter
points, from the weighting arrangement alone, with names and trade count held fixed. The new
control is that same effect with a steeper and stickier ladder behind it.

Two more things the shape check does not match, and neither is small:

- How much money is actually invested. The control sits 88% invested against the book's 81%.
  Six points more money at work, over twenty years, is worth a few tenths of a point by
  itself.
- What the trading costs. At turnover matched to within one per cent, the control still paid
  about one and a half times the book's fee bill, because turnover is measured as money
  moved against average equity while the broker charges a minimum per order, and the control
  spreads the same money across differently-sized trades.

So: matching trades and turnover is necessary and it is demonstrably not sufficient. A
control that matches both can still differ from its book in how it holds its money, how much
of it is invested, and what it pays to trade. When a margin against a shape-matched control
is published, say which of those three were matched and which were not.
