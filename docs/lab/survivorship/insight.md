**The answer: on average the missing dead companies barely moved our results, about half a point a year, but they did hide deeper falls in the momentum strategies.** Until now every strategy was tested on a price history that was missing about four in ten of the days a company sat in the big US indexes, mostly the companies that later went bankrupt, were bought or disappeared. This month we bought that missing history, cleaned it carefully (real collapses kept in, data errors taken out), and re-ran 102 versions of 22 strategies on both histories with the same money and the same monthly top-ups. Every version first reproduced its old result exactly on the old history, so the differences come only from the added companies. Nothing was added to the lab's trial count and no hidden-years look was spent.

**Buying long-term losers was the sharpest test.** This strategy buys the big companies that fell furthest over the past three to five years. If dead companies were hiding anywhere, it would be here, because the worst losers are exactly the ones that went on to die. On the fuller history its best version earned 7.3% a year instead of 9.8% (SPY: 7.2%), and its version with a trend filter earned 8.5% instead of 9.8%, with its worst fall going from 34% to 39%. Two of its five versions no longer beat SPY, and the other three now beat it by about a point or less. It won only one of its four walk-forward periods instead of two. The missing dead companies were flattering this idea by about one and a half points a year.

**The momentum books** (26 versions) lost 1.1 points a year on average (from 1.8 points worse to 0.2 better). Two of them stopped beating SPY, both of which had been within a point of it. The bigger change is the worst fall: it got deeper by 2.6 points on average, and nine versions that had stayed inside the 20% worst-fall line went over it. That includes our best momentum book, which still grows 13.6% a year against SPY's 7.9%, but whose worst fall went from 19.6% to 23.5%.
**The momentum plus calm-stock blends** (4 versions) lost about 1 point a year; one stopped beating SPY, and none crossed the 20% line.
**The earnings-jump book** (5 versions) lost 0.6 points a year on average, and it now wins only one of its four walk-forward periods instead of three.
**The dividend-date strategies** (62 versions) barely moved, 0.1 points a year on average. One stopped beating SPY and one started; two crossed the 20% worst-fall line. Their results stand as they were.

**Did any verdict change?** Yes, in three ways. Six versions stopped beating SPY and one started; all six had been less than two points a year ahead of SPY. Eleven versions that kept their worst fall inside 20% now fall further than that, and none moved the other way, which matters because a 20% worst fall is one of the bars for real money. And several strategies won fewer of their four walk-forward periods: the earnings-jump book and the dividend-cut alarm went from three to one, two momentum strategies and one blend from three to two, and long-term losers from two to one.

**How the fuller history looks.** It prices 89% of the index days instead of 59%. 2009-2015 is now 97 to 99% covered, against 65 to 76% before. 111 members are still missing even after we searched for their old ticker codes, about 5% of the index days, so a small part of the hole remains.

**What remains uncertain.** 1996 and 1997 are still thin (under half the days priced), because the bought histories for dead companies mostly start on the last day of 1997. Any edge that lives in those two years is not checked. The 111 members still missing are mostly companies whose ticker a different company now uses, so a little survivorship bias is still left in both histories.

**What this changes in how we work.** The fuller history stays a second opinion. It does not decide which strategy may be tried on the hidden years. That rule changes only through a separate, argued change, made after reading these numbers. Every future strategy can be checked against it, and the next batch should do so before trusting any momentum result whose worst fall sits just under 20%, or any result built on buying fallen or troubled companies.
